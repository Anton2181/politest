    How the maps are made

    • Each scenario is a cohort-component projection for 23 regions: the 17 Polish voivodeships of 1931 and 6 Lithuanian units, split rural/urban, with 43 community–language groups. The maps place those regional numbers on 9,148 cells of about 7 × 7 km, so every region always adds up to the model.
    • 1932. County language shares from the 1931 Polish census (and estimates where the census was silent, such as Kashubian, Lemko and the Lauda gentry) are interpolated inside each voivodeship and fitted to its totals by iterative proportional fitting.
    • Each year. The region's net language shift is placed where speakers of the gaining language live nearby. Following Prochazka & Vogl, the chance to shift grows with the gaining language's share in a Gaussian neighbourhood (10 km), plus a constant part for school, church and state. Towns reach further into their hinterland as they grow.
    • Population. Towns follow the transport-network model. Rural cells near growing towns gain a little each year; remote ones lose, which produces suburban rings and rural exodus.
    • Transport. The network grows by cost–benefit appraisal under rail and road budgets, plus the dated 1930s projects and the 1939 plans. Lines are drawn straight between the 230 modelled towns, so they show connections, not alignments. The map shows the network as of the latest 5-year frame.
    • County level. The two "county level" scenarios run the projection for about 270 counties (powiaty and Lithuanian apskritys) instead of 23 regions. The eight eastern voivodeships start from the 1931 county tables of mother tongue; the others are downscaled estimates. Faint lines mark county borders.
    • Federal members. Dashed lines mark the borders between federal members, such as Poland and Lithuania, or the autonomy and the cantons in the cantonal scenario. Within a member, each sub-region has its own official language.

    Reading with care

    • The maps show the modelled home language, not what a census would print. The 1931 census counted many Belarusian, Ukrainian and Polesian speakers as Polish or "local". The two "1931 start" scenarios begin from alternative reconstructions.
    • Internal borders are approximations (a weighted Voronoi of county towns). A 7 km cell averages over several villages, so a single Lauda manor or the Karaim quarter of Troki is below the grid's resolution.
    • Towns take their region's urban language mix, tilted toward their hinterland. Shtetls with Jewish majorities, such as Pińsk or Brody, therefore look more mixed than they were.
    • Paler colours in the language layer mean the leading language has a thin plurality, below about half of residents.
