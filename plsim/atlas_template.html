    How the maps are made

    • Each scenario is a cohort-component projection for about 270 counties: the 1931 powiaty and the Lithuanian apskritys, split rural/urban, with 43 community–language groups. The eight eastern voivodeships start from the 1931 county tables of mother tongue; the other counties are downscaled from their voivodeship. In Wakar's Poland-Belarus, Soviet Belarus adds four voivodeships made of the 12 okrugs of the 1926 Soviet census. The maps place those county numbers on cells of 3.5 × 3.4 km (about 37,500 for Poland and Lithuania), so every county always adds up to the model.
    • 1932. County language shares from the 1931 Polish census (and estimates where the census was silent, such as Kashubian, Lemko and the Lauda gentry) are interpolated inside each voivodeship and fitted to its totals by iterative proportional fitting.
    • Each year. The region's net language shift is placed where speakers of the gaining language live nearby. Following Prochazka & Vogl, the chance to shift grows with the gaining language's share in a Gaussian neighbourhood (10 km), plus a constant part for school, church and state. Towns reach further into their hinterland as they grow.
    • Population. Towns follow the transport-network model. Rural cells near growing towns gain a little each year; remote ones lose, which produces suburban rings and rural exodus.
    • Transport. The network grows by cost–benefit appraisal under rail and road budgets, plus the dated 1930s projects and the 1939 plans. Lines are drawn straight between the modelled towns (about 200, with gateways abroad), so they show connections, not alignments. The map shows the network as of the latest 5-year frame.
    • Zoom. Scroll, pinch or use the buttons to zoom; drag to pan. Faint lines mark county borders, stronger ones voivodeships. County names and more towns appear as you zoom in.
    • Federal members and official languages. Dashed lines mark the borders between federal members, such as Poland and Lithuania, or the autonomies and cantons. Pointing at a cell shows its county's official language(s). Where several are co-official, the first is the county's working language.
    • Whole areas. Click or tap the map to select the county, voivodeship or federal member (canton, autonomy, Poland, Lithuania) under the pointer. The panel then shows its residents, growth since 1932, area, density and home languages for the year shown, and the chart follows the selection. The totals add up the map cells, so they match the model to rounding.
    • Curzon line. The overlay draws, for each 5-year frame, a continuous line across the state, between two points of its outer border (Lithuania and Soviet Belarus included where they are part of the state), that leaves as many non-Poles on its Polish side as Poles on its other side, and among such lines keeps the most Poles on the Polish side. The line may wind cell by cell on the 3.5 km grid, through counties; each side is in one piece. Equivalently, the Polish side holds exactly as many counted people as there are Poles. It is found by growing the Polish side from its most Polish cells and then exchanging cells along the line. Poles are speakers of Polish at home; Kashubians, Wymysorys speakers, Germans and Jews are not counted at all.
    • Left-out land. In the two Wakar scenarios (without the south-east and Wilno), the land outside the state is shown in the neutral colour. Soviet Belarus is foreign land except in Wakar's Poland-Belarus.

    Reading with care

    • The maps show the modelled home language, not what a census would print. The 1931 census counted many Belarusian, Ukrainian and Polesian speakers as Polish or "local". The two "1931 start" scenarios begin from alternative reconstructions.
    • The state borders are those of 1 January 1932 (CShapes 2.0). Voivodeship and county borders are approximations (weighted Voronoi diagrams of the towns and county seats). A 3.5 km cell still averages over a few villages, so a single Lauda manor or the Karaim quarter of Troki is below the grid's resolution.
    • Towns take their region's urban language mix, tilted toward their hinterland. Shtetls with Jewish majorities, such as Pińsk or Brody, therefore look more mixed than they were.
    • Paler colours in the language layer mean the leading language has a thin plurality, below about half of residents.
